U.S. Moves to Restrict Some Foreign-Made Inverters and Battery Equipment

Published August 2026 · SolarRateHub

The United States is taking additional steps to secure its bulk-power system, with recent policy action covering categories of equipment used to generate, transmit, distribute and manage electricity. The developments are relevant to the solar industry because grid-connected inverters and battery energy-storage equipment can be part of the broader power-system technology supply chain.

Why solar installers should care: Equipment sourcing, certification, cybersecurity requirements, project timelines and supplier selection could become increasingly important for solar and storage projects that connect to the U.S. grid.

What Is Changing?

Recent U.S. policy measures are aimed at reducing security risks associated with certain bulk-power equipment produced or supplied by foreign entities. The scope and practical effect can depend on the equipment category, project configuration, applicable rules and implementation guidance.

What Does This Mean for Solar Inverters?

Solar inverters sit between photovoltaic generation, batteries and the electrical grid. For larger grid-connected systems, inverter technology can therefore become part of the equipment-security discussion. This does not mean that every foreign-made inverter is automatically prohibited. Buyers and developers should distinguish between a policy scope, a specific restriction and an outright ban.

For homeowners and installers, the practical takeaway is to check the latest manufacturer documentation, certification status, sourcing information and utility requirements before selecting equipment for a new project.

Impact on Battery Energy Storage

Battery energy-storage systems are increasingly important for solar projects because they can shift solar production, provide backup power and support grid services. Policies affecting power-system equipment may therefore have implications for battery systems and their associated power-conversion equipment.

Could Prices Increase?

It is too early to assume a uniform price increase across all solar inverters or batteries. Pricing depends on supply, manufacturing location, tariffs, certification, inventory, demand and individual project requirements. If sourcing restrictions become broader, however, the industry could see changes in supplier availability and procurement costs.

What Solar Buyers Should Do

  • Check current U.S. certification and grid-interconnection requirements.
  • Ask suppliers where critical equipment is manufactured.
  • Keep documentation for inverter and battery model numbers.
  • Confirm that proposed equipment meets the applicable utility and electrical-code requirements.
  • For larger projects, review cybersecurity and supply-chain requirements with qualified professionals.
Important: This article is an informational news summary, not legal, regulatory or engineering advice. U.S. energy policy can change quickly. Always verify the latest federal guidance and manufacturer requirements before making equipment or procurement decisions.
