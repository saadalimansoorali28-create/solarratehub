Why the price per kW is useful

Solar modules come in different wattages, so comparing the sticker price of one panel against another can be misleading. A 500-watt panel naturally costs more than a 400-watt panel even when the underlying module price is similar.

Converting the price to dollars per kilowatt gives homeowners and solar shoppers a cleaner apples-to-apples comparison. For example, $0.45/W equals $450/kW.

What these prices include — and what they do not

The numbers in this article are intended to represent the solar panel or module only. They do not include an inverter, battery storage, racking, wiring, labor, permits, sales tax, shipping or installation.

That distinction matters because a panel-only price can look very low compared with the final price of a professionally installed residential solar system. The complete project includes many components and soft costs beyond the modules.

How to read the September 2026 comparison

Trina, Jinko and Canadian Solar examples sit close together in this comparison on a per-kW basis. REC is positioned higher, reflecting the premium pricing of the example selected. The Qcells figure is specifically a domestic-content example and should not be treated as the price of every Qcells module sold in America.

In real shopping situations, the cheapest module is not automatically the best value. Warranty terms, efficiency, available roof area, temperature performance, degradation, distributor support and the installer’s final design can all matter.

Bottom line

For September 1–14, 2026, the indicative U.S. panel-only comparison puts the lower-priced examples around $450–$470 per kW, while premium examples in this list are around $670–$880 per kW.

If you are comparing quotes, ask the installer to separate the module cost from the rest of the project. That makes it much easier to see whether you are paying for the panels themselves or for installation and the other equipment required to turn those panels into a working solar system.
